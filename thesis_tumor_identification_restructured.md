# Tumor Cell Identification

Identifying tumor cells in a metastatic brain section is non-trivial: the 958-gene CosMx panel does not include a single definitive marker for the D122 lung carcinoma line, and transcriptional similarity between metastatic carcinoma cells and certain stressed, proliferating, or atypical host populations can blur the boundary between tumor and non-tumor cells in expression space [30, 31]. To address this, tumor cells were identified using a three-stage approach: an initial reference-based annotation with SingleR [32] using a Lewis Lung Carcinoma reference [33]; a supervised classifier trained on high-confidence positive and negative reference cells derived from the data itself; and application of the selected classifier to re-score all tumor candidates across the six slices.

## Stage 1 — Reference-based annotation with SingleR

Initial cell-type annotation was performed using SingleR (version 2.12.0; R 4.5) [32]. SingleR is a reference-based annotation method that, by default, labels each query cell independently without requiring prior clustering. For each cell, it computes Spearman correlations between the cell's expression profile and the profiles of labelled reference samples, using marker genes identified from pairwise comparisons between reference labels. When a reference label is represented by multiple samples, a query cell yields a distribution of correlations for that label. For each candidate label, SingleR summarizes the resulting distribution of correlations using a fixed quantile (the 0.8 quantile by default). This emphasizes relatively strong matches while reducing sensitivity to individual extreme reference samples. The label with the highest score provides the initial assignment. By default, SingleR then performs fine-tuning: labels with scores close to the maximum are retained, marker genes that discriminate among these remaining labels are identified, and the scores are recalculated. This process is repeated until a final label is selected. Fine-tuning can improve discrimination between transcriptionally similar cell types. SingleR reports the predicted label, scores for the reference labels, and diagnostic measures such as the difference between the best and next-best fine-tuned scores. It can also prune ambiguous or low-quality assignments, reporting them as unassigned. These outputs indicate the relative support for an annotation but should not be interpreted as calibrated probabilities of correctness.

SingleR was applied with a reference panel we assembled from external datasets. It comprised three components: (i) a brain structural reference covering astrocytes, neurons, oligodendrocytes, vascular cells, and other stromal CNS populations; (ii) a brain immune reference covering microglia, BAMs, and infiltrating myeloid populations; and (iii) a tumor-specific reference derived from a publicly available bulk RNA-seq dataset of LLC1 (Lewis Lung Carcinoma) cells (Gene Expression Omnibus accession GSE103548) [33]. D122 is a highly metastatic subclone of the parental Lewis Lung Carcinoma lineage [14] and shares its core carcinoma transcriptional program; no single-cell reference for D122 specifically was publicly available at the time of analysis, so LLC1 was used as the closest available proxy. This choice captures the lung-carcinoma identity shared by the two lines, while D122-specific features — particularly those acquired during brain-metastatic adaptation — are not represented in the reference. The supervised classifier described in Stage 2 is trained on D122 cells from this experiment, so its decision boundary reflects their observed expression rather than the LLC1 profile alone.

### Sets derived from the SingleR output

For each segmented cell, SingleR returned correlation-based scores against each reference (`score_brain_struct`, `score_brain_immune`, `score_tumor`) and the predicted label corresponding to the highest-scoring class. Cells whose top label was "Tumor" were retained for further analysis, subject to three filters designed to exclude weakly-matching calls: an absolute score floor, a margin between the best and second-best reference score, and a best-class consistency check. Threshold values are listed in Table 3 and their calibration is described at the end of this section.

Three sets were derived from this output, each serving a different purpose.

**Tumor candidates** are the cells passing those three filters, in all six sections. They are the cells the supervised classifier re-scores. The thresholds defining them are deliberately permissive: the candidate pool is not intended to be an accurate set of tumor calls, but to be inclusive enough that few true tumor cells are discarded before refinement. Final specificity is the responsibility of the classifier.

**Tumor anchors** answer a different need. Training the classifier requires a positive class that can be trusted, and the candidate pool cannot supply one, precisely because it contains the errors the classifier exists to remove. Anchors were therefore defined by applying the same three filters with a stricter `score_tumor` floor, retaining only those cells whose tumor identity is least ambiguous. Because the floor is stricter, the anchors form a subset of the candidate pool. They were drawn from slice 1 (L321) and slice 5 (L34).

**Healthy look-alikes** provide the negative training class, and are obtained by applying the candidate filters to the two sham-injected control sections (slice 3 for L321, slice 4 for L34). Because no metastatic cells are present in control tissue, every cell selected there is by construction a healthy cell that SingleR has misassigned — exactly the kind of false positive the classifier must learn to reject.

### Why refinement was necessary

Visual inspection of the spatial distribution of SingleR-called tumor cells revealed two systematic issues. First, a substantial number of cells annotated as "Tumor" were located in the sham-injected control sections, where no metastatic cells can be present by experimental design. Second, additional "Tumor" calls appeared in distal, clearly non-tumor anatomical regions of the tumor-bearing sections. Because D122 cells were stereotactically injected into the contralateral hemisphere and tumors developed as locally compact lesions, these distributed calls are unlikely to represent true metastatic cells. They instead reflect transcriptional overlap between D122-like signal and certain endogenous brain populations within the limited 958-gene panel: the panel cannot, on its own, fully discriminate tumor cells from a subset of host look-alike cells. Reference-based annotation alone was therefore insufficient, and a supervised refinement step was developed, trained on the specific failure modes of SingleR in this dataset.

## Stage 2 — Supervised refinement of tumor candidates

A supervised binary classification framework was used to distinguish true tumor cells from look-alike host cells incorrectly annotated as tumor by SingleR. The positive class comprised the tumor anchors and the negative class the healthy look-alikes, both defined in Stage 1; the joint reference pool contained 1,493 cells (863 anchors, 630 look-alikes). Labels follow the convention that *y* = 1 denotes the healthy look-alike class, so the classifier reports *P*(healthy) and the tumor call is its complement.

Five classifier configurations were evaluated: (1) L2-regularized logistic regression on the full gene space; (2) logistic regression in a 50-component PCA-reduced space; (3) a hybrid logistic-regression + k-nearest-neighbor classifier operating in the same PCA space, in which a cell was considered healthy if either the logistic probability or the fraction of healthy k-NN neighbors exceeded the operating threshold; (4) random forest and (5) gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost) on the full gene space. The XGBoost model was used with its library default hyperparameters; no tuning was performed (Table 3). The remaining configurations used scikit-learn defaults except for raised iteration limits for convergence, 300 trees in the random forest, 50 principal components, and 15 neighbours in the k-NN component. Class balancing was not applied to any model: the reference pool is only mildly imbalanced (863:630), and weighting some models but not others would make the comparison asymmetric.

All five classifiers achieved high and broadly comparable performance on the joint reference pool under 5-fold cross-validation (Figure 3; Table 4), with out-of-fold ROC-AUC above 0.99 and average precision above 0.98 across all configurations. This near-uniformity is a consequence of the high purity of both training classes by construction — the positive set comprises high-confidence tumor anchors and the negative set experimentally-impossible control-section calls — which makes the discrimination close to trivial for any reasonable model. The differences between configurations fall within the resampling interval and do not identify a best model. The operationally meaningful comparison is therefore not the cross-validated reference-set metrics, but the classifiers' behaviour on the candidate pool: cells with intermediate or ambiguous tumor signal, flagged for refinement, that the reference-set metrics say nothing about.

On the candidate pool the classifiers diverge in ways the reference-pool metrics do not predict. Retention of candidates in tumor-bearing tissue ranged from 63.4% (logistic regression with k-NN) to 71.9% (logistic regression in PCA space), with XGBoost at 68.6%, logistic regression at 66.7% and random forest at 65.8%. More decisively, only three configurations — logistic regression, random forest and XGBoost — returned zero tumor calls in the sham-injected control sections; logistic regression in PCA space retained 14 control-section false positives and the logistic + k-NN hybrid retained 3. Inspection of the spatial distribution of retained tumor cells (Figure 4) showed that XGBoost preserved coherent tumor masses with peripheral false positives cleanly removed, whereas logistic regression additionally removed substantial fractions of cells within the dense tumor core, likely reflecting transcriptional heterogeneity that a linear decision boundary could not capture.

XGBoost was selected as the final classifier on this basis: it was one of only three configurations achieving perfect control-section specificity, and of those three it retained the largest fraction of candidates in tumor-bearing tissue while preserving spatially coherent tumor masses. Its non-linear decision function and its capacity to model interactions between panel genes are better suited to the heterogeneity of the candidate pool than the linear or PCA-projected alternatives. Random forest behaved similarly in space but retained fewer candidates throughout.

## Stage 3 — Final tumor calls

The selected XGBoost model was applied to all SingleR-derived tumor candidates across the six analyzed sections. For each candidate the model returned a probability *P*(healthy); cells with *P*(healthy) < 0.5 were retained as refined tumor cells, and cells with *P*(healthy) ≥ 0.5 were reclassified as look-alike host cells and excluded from downstream tumor-centric analyses. This yielded 20,873 refined tumor cells across the four tumor-bearing sections and none in either control section.

Spatial inspection of the refined population (Figure 4) confirmed that rejected look-alike cells were concentrated in the control sections and in distal non-tumor regions of the tumor-bearing sections, while retained tumor cells formed compact, spatially coherent clusters consistent with the histologically observed tumor masses.

## Threshold calibration

The three Stage-1 thresholds — the candidate `score_tumor` floor, the `delta_score` margin, and the stricter `score_tumor` floor defining the anchors — have no library default and were calibrated empirically against the pipeline output.

SingleR provides a default call-quality filter (`pruneScores`, based on median-absolute-deviation outlier detection of the per-label score delta) but no absolute score cut-off, and its scores are 0.8-quantile Spearman correlations rather than calibrated probabilities, so no universal cut-off such as 0.5 applies: the median `score_tumor` among Tumor-called cells was 0.16–0.21 across the six sections, and a 0.5 cut-off would have retained 240 of 209,081 calls (0.12%). The two sham-injected control sections contain no tumor, so every cell the pipeline assigns to the tumor class in those sections is a false positive by construction, providing a directly observable error rate against which thresholds can be calibrated. Each threshold was swept across a range of values and each setting carried through to the final refined tumor set, so that sensitivity was assessed on the pipeline output rather than on the intermediate candidate count. For the candidate thresholds the classifier was held fixed and only the set of cells presented to it varied; for the anchor floor, which defines the positive training class, the classifier was retrained at each value.

Sweeping the candidate `score_tumor` floor from 0.10 to 0.30 showed that a floor of 0.2 was the most permissive setting at which both control sections yielded zero refined tumor calls; floors of 0.15 and 0.10 each admitted one false positive, while a floor of 0.25 removed 21.6% of the refined tumor set (20,873 to 16,357 cells) without improving control-tissue specificity, which was already perfect. The `delta_score` margin behaved equivalently: a margin of 0.08 was the smallest value at which control tissue returned zero refined tumor calls, whereas margins of 0.06, 0.04 and 0 left 9, 16 and 31 false positives respectively, and a margin of 0.10 discarded a further 25.4% of the refined set. Both candidate thresholds were retained at the boundary of the zero-false-positive region: loosening either admitted false positives in control tissue, tightening either cost 21–25% of the refined tumor set for no specificity gain.

The stricter floor defining the tumor anchors required a different criterion, because every floor from 0.30 to 0.50 gave zero refined tumor calls in control tissue and the zero-false-positive rule cannot distinguish between them. It was set instead by the purity of the anchor set, measured on the SingleR scores alone, before the classifier is applied. Anchors are drawn only from the tumor-bearing sections, but the same filter can be applied to the control sections as a check, where any cell it selects is a look-alike by construction: it selected 115 control cells at a floor of 0.30, 31 at 0.35, 5 at 0.40 and none at 0.45, corresponding to anchor purities of 99.04%, 99.41%, 99.76% and 100%. Because the classifier was retrained at each floor, the effect on the output can be read directly. Lowering the floor below 0.40 admits look-alikes into the training set at a measurably higher rate, and the refined tumor set inflates accordingly: by 20.6% at a floor of 0.35 and 37.6% at 0.30. Raising it above 0.40 buys at most 0.24 percentage points of purity while collapsing the anchor set from 863 cells to 334 at 0.45, discarding 38.3% of the refined tumor set. A floor of 0.40 was adopted as the point at which purity has effectively saturated while the anchor set remains large enough to train on. Out-of-fold AUROC rose steadily across this range (0.974 to 0.999), but this reflects the surviving anchors becoming progressively more extreme and therefore easier to separate; it does not indicate a better model, and was not used to select the floor.
